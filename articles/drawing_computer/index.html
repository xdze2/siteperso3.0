Computer drawing & etching render

Introductory course, notes form. How a computer turns an image (photo, 3D scene, depth map) into something that reads as drawn: ink or no ink, lines, dots, hatching.

Field name: NPR, non-photorealistic rendering. Mostly 1990s–2000s SIGGRAPH papers, then pen plotters and shaders. The starting point is older: la gravure en taille directe is binary. Ink or no ink. Every grey is optical. Same problem as a 1-bit screen or a plotter with one pen.

Sommaire

  • 1. Tone value
    Black and white → grey. Threshold, quantization, dithering (ordered, error diffusion), halftone, stippling. How the eye averages.
  • 2. Hatching & engraving
    Tone from lines. Spacing, width, cross-hatching. Adding orientation (direction fields, streamlines, curvature). Adding texture (grain, roughness, Tonal Art Maps). Burin vs algorithm.
  • 3. Stylized drawing
    Line drawing: edges, silhouettes, contours, suggestive contours. Line weight, sketchy strokes. Depth perception: atmospheric perspective in lines, indication, repoussoir.
  • 4. Perception: encoding volume and depth
    The illusion on a flat sheet. Cues for volume (shading, contour, line direction) and for depth (occlusion, texture gradient, cast shadows, atmospheric perspective, level of detail), by artists and by algorithms. Then which information goes into which property of the marks, and methods as filters.
  • References
    Papers, books, software, artists, image sources. DOIs checked.

Le fil rouge

Three pieces of information to carry with ink only:

  1. tone — how dark (density of ink),
  2. orientation — which way the surface turns (direction of strokes),
  3. texture — what the surface is made of (character of strokes).

Plus the contours, which say where things stop, and depth, which says what is far. Each chapter adds one layer.

First version. Images are computed demos (Python, numpy) or public-domain prints from Wikimedia Commons. Papers linked, not reproduced.
